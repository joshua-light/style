Three films: a lock, a heap, a boss

Each film explains one mechanism, and each is built on a different kind of motion: parts that ride, pour, spread, sweep, close ranks, race, and swap places.

A canal, a garbage collector, a raid · about 3 minutes · 3 films

A lock lifts a boat with gates and gravity

Moves. One part rides another: the boat sits on the water. A stream thins as the flow slows. The level loses speed as it nears its goal, as real water does.

A canal cannot climb a hill, so it climbs in steps. At each step sits a lock: a chamber of water with a gate at either end.

On a real canal the fill takes a few minutes. The lift is paid for in water: emptying the chamber for the next boat sends one chamberful downhill for good.

A garbage collector looks for the living

Moves. A mark spreads along the wires, wave by wave. A cursor wipes what it passes. The survivors close ranks, and their wires follow them.

A program makes objects all day and never says when it is done with one. The collector has to work that out. The natural guess is that it hunts for garbage. It does the opposite.

The scheme is called mark and compact, and the collectors in .NET and Java are built on it. They add generations and work in the background, but the test stays the same: can a root still reach it?

A boss turns only when a margin is beaten

Moves. Bars race at a steady pace. Rows swap places. A tick rides another bar's value. A 3-second timer drains in 3 seconds. The stage is the style's own meter, not a drawing.

In World of Warcraft a boss keeps a score for every player in the fight: threat. A raid lives by one rule about that score, and the rule has a catch.

The margin is the catch, and it is a kindness. A damage dealer who edges past the tank gets a warning, not a death. A tank who loses the boss gets one button that buys 3 seconds.
